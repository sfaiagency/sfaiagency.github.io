Product

AI training audit

In more and more places, AI companies must publish a summary of what they trained on. Many must also have a policy for respecting creators who opt out. But a published statement is only a claim.

We check whether the claim is true. We compare what a company says about its training data with what is actually in the data and the models. We use the same technology we built to find creative work inside AI training data.

Our audit reports are for regulators and rights organizations that need to test a company’s claims. They are also for AI companies that want to check their own reports, or a supplier’s, before they publish.

What we check

The disclosures

Does the published summary match the training data?

  • We compare a company’s published training-data summary with independent evidence of what the model was trained on.
  • We look for gaps: sources, copyrighted work, or personal data that should have been listed and were not.
  • We look for work and datasets that show up in the model but not in the paperwork.
  • Every gap comes with evidence that anyone can re-run.

The policies

Does the company do what its policy says?

  • Opt-outs: when creators and publishers say no to text and data mining, does their work stay out of training?
  • Markets: does the company meet the rules of each place it sells in, not only its home market?
  • Privacy: is scraped data handled the way privacy rules on notice require?
  • Over time: we run the audit again when models, datasets, or rules change.

Standards we audit against

European Union

AI Act, Article 53

Providers of general-purpose AI models must publish a sufficiently detailed summary of their training content, using the AI Office’s template. They must also keep a copyright policy that respects rights reservations. In force since August 2025. Enforcement and fines start in August 2026.

European Union

DSM Copyright Directive, Article 4

Text and data mining is only allowed where rights holders have not reserved their rights. That makes a copyright policy testable: reserved works either stay out of training or they don’t.

California

AB 2013: Training Data Transparency

Since January 2026, developers of generative AI systems offered in California must publish information about their training datasets. That includes sources, copyrighted material, and personal information. The disclosure must stay current as systems change.

Australia

Privacy Act reforms

From December 2026, organizations covered by the Australian Privacy Principles must say in their privacy policies what kinds of personal information they use in substantially automated decisions. The Privacy Commissioner already gives guidance on AI and personal data.

New Zealand

Privacy Act 2020 and IPP 3A

New Zealand’s privacy principles apply across the AI lifecycle. From May 2026, the new IPP 3A requires agencies that collect personal information indirectly, the way training data is usually gathered, to tell people about it.

We add new rules as they arrive. We give independent technical findings, not legal advice.
